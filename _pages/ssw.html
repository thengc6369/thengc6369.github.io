My research on Earth's atmosphere has focused on the dynamics and triggering mechanisms of stratospheric sudden warmings (SSWs), particularly events involving the splitting of the winter polar vortex. I have investigated these phenomena using stochastic and dynamical models, atmospheric reanalysis data, and idealized general circulation model (GCM) simulations.

Stochastic triggering of vortex-splitting SSWs

Using a stochastic extension of Kida's elliptical vortex model, we investigated how random tropospheric wave forcing can trigger vortex-splitting SSWs through nonlinear resonance. We demonstrated that the polar vortex undergoes a random walk through its dynamical phase space, potentially reaching a critical state at which vortex splitting occurs. This provides a mechanism for SSWs that does not require an exceptionally strong planetary wave event immediately before their onset. Instead, the history of the forcing and the resulting preconditioning of the vortex play a central role. We also derived analytical estimates of the expected time until vortex splitting using stochastic first-passage theory.

Related publication (QJRMS, 2019)

Dynamics of the Antarctic polar vortex

I investigated the observed evolution of the Antarctic stratospheric polar vortex using elliptical vortex diagnostics and a variational data assimilation method based on Kida's vortex model. Applying this framework to atmospheric reanalysis data from 1999–2018, we identified large interannual differences in vortex oscillations and reconstructed the background flow responsible for its deformation. In particular, we demonstrated that the exceptional Antarctic SSW of 2002 was associated with the vortex crossing a critical boundary in dynamical phase space. Our results also revealed the relative importance of stationary planetary wave forcing and time-dependent tropospheric disturbances in controlling vortex evolution.

Related publication (Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences, 2020)

Triggering mechanisms of SSWs in idealized GCM simulations

Using long integrations of an idealized atmospheric GCM, I have investigated how the strength and structure of the stratospheric polar vortex influence the occurrence of vortex-splitting SSWs. By varying the planetary rotation rate and topographic forcing, we examined how changes in the background circulation modify vortex stability and SSW frequency. A particular focus has been on distinguishing events triggered by anomalously strong upward planetary wave activity from those in which the pre-existing stratospheric state plays a more important role. Our results highlight the importance of vortex preconditioning and suggest that not all SSWs require an exceptional burst of tropospheric wave forcing immediately before their onset.
